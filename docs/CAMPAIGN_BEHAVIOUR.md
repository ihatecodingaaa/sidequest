# Behavioural design of ONE BAD MINUTE

Written so the psychology teammate can read it and disagree with it.

Each chapter states its mechanism, the hypothesis it rests on, what it is
trying to achieve, what a pilot could measure, how well evidenced the mechanism
is, and what it does not show. The last two columns matter most: the product
should not overclaim, and neither should this document.

A note on evidence status throughout: **grounded** means the mechanism is
well established in the literature and the chapter applies it in a
recognisable way. **Plausible** means the mechanism is established but the
application here is our own design choice. **Untested** means nobody has run
this and we do not know.

---

## Chapter 1: Quick money (REWIND)

**Mechanism.** Decision rehearsal and implementation intentions, combined with
bystander intervention.

**Hypothesis.** People rarely fail these moments because they do not know what
the right answer is. They fail because they have never said the sentence out
loud, and the moment is over in under a minute. Rehearsing a specific response
in a specific situation makes it retrievable at speed.

**Objective.** Practise a low-conflict intervention while a situation is still
easy to reverse.

**Design detail that carries the argument.** The options are graded by how much
face they cost the other person, not by moral force. The two that produce good
outcomes are asking to check the number (a task, not an accusation) and walking
the friend away from the group (removing the audience). Saying it publicly is
modelled honestly as *harder*, not as wrong: it converts a decision into a
status contest the other person cannot lose gracefully.

**Measurement opportunity.** Which pivot option is chosen on the first run
versus the second. A shift towards low-face-cost options after the rewind is
the thing the chapter is trying to produce.

**Evidence status.** Grounded for implementation intentions and for the
bystander finding that people act more readily once someone else moves first.
Plausible for the specific face-cost ordering, which is our design judgement
informed by that literature rather than a replicated result.

**Limitations.** One rehearsal in a fictional scenario is not a behaviour
change. At most this makes a response available; whether it is used under real
social pressure is untested. The scenario also gives the participant the
observer role, which is the easiest role in the room.

## Chapter 2: Everyone would do it (Norm Mirror)

**Mechanism.** Perceived versus reported social norms, and pluralistic
ignorance.

**Hypothesis.** People overestimate how many of their peers would take a risky
option, and the overestimate is itself part of what makes the option feel
normal. Making the gap visible reduces the sense that the risky choice is the
default.

**Objective.** Challenge the assumption that a risky choice is automatically
the socially normal one, without telling anybody what to do.

**Design detail that carries the argument.** The prediction is collected
**before** the personal choice. Reversing that order would let the prediction be
rationalised backwards to match what the participant just admitted, and the gap
would stop meaning anything.

**Measurement opportunity.** The single cleanest measure in the whole Campaign:
predicted peer rate against reported rate, per question, per cohort. Also the
number of questions on which an individual overestimated by a meaningful
margin.

**Evidence status.** Grounded for the existence and direction of the
misperception. Social norms interventions have a real but mixed track record,
and they work best when the corrected norm is **local** and credible to the
audience.

**Limitations, and the important one.** *Every percentage in this build is
invented.* They are placeholders that demonstrate the mechanism and they are
not findings about Singapore youth. The product labels them "Demo aggregate"
and "Prototype data" on every reveal.

This matters more than a disclaimer usually would, because a social norms
intervention derives its entire effect from the audience believing the number.
Showing a real cohort an invented figure would be both ineffective and
dishonest. The first thing a pilot must do is replace them with that cohort's
own answers. See `docs/PILOT_PLAN.md`.

## Chapter 3: Design the moment (BREAKSAFE)

**Mechanism.** Situational crime prevention and choice architecture.

**Hypothesis.** A large share of self-checkout loss is unintentional, produced
by ambiguous feedback and the social cost of asking for help, rather than by
intent. Changing the environment therefore addresses more of the problem than
identifying people does, and it does so without profiling anybody.

**Objective.** Understand that prevention can redesign a system instead of
watching individuals.

**Design detail that carries the argument.** Facial recognition is offered as a
patch option and scores 1 out of 5 on privacy and fairness, with the reasoning
spelled out. Refusing it after reading the scores is a stronger lesson than
never being offered it. Two hotspots are decoys and explain why they are not
the answer: the overhead camera (which documents the problem after it happens)
and the shopper (who is never the thing to solve).

**Measurement opportunity.** Which patches are selected, and specifically the
proportion of participants who select the profiling option before and after
seeing its scores.

**Evidence status.** Grounded. Situational crime prevention in the Clarke
tradition is a well developed body of work, and the distinction between
deliberate theft and unintentional non-scanning is standard in retail loss
research.

**Limitations.** The mock terminal is a fiction built to teach, not a real
system with real telemetry. The chapter shows a participant that design
choices matter; it does not show that any particular patch would work.

## Chapter 4: Crew Shift

**Mechanism.** Peer discussion, private commitment before group reveal, and
collective decision-making.

**Hypothesis.** Peer influence is normally invisible, including to the people
being influenced. Making everyone commit privately before anyone speaks
produces a clean before and after on the same group, and lets a group see its
own movement.

**Objective.** Make peer influence visible, and practise disagreeing with
friends constructively.

**Design detail that carries the argument.** No score, no correct answer, and
four defensible options with honestly stated trade-offs. The discussion timer
is a prompt rather than a constraint: it is skippable, and running out does
nothing. If the timer had teeth it would push groups towards whoever talks
fastest, which is the opposite of the point.

**Measurement opportunity.** The proportion of groups whose final decision
differs from the majority of their private answers, and in which direction.
Also whether group size correlates with movement.

**Evidence status.** Grounded for the underlying phenomena (conformity, group
polarisation, the effect of private versus public commitment). Untested as an
intervention in this specific form: whether a 45 second structured argument
produces anything durable is not something we know.

**Limitations, and one worth saying plainly.** The chapter measures
**movement, not improvement.** A group shifting is not automatically a good
outcome, and the product deliberately does not score it as one. A group could
just as easily talk itself into the passive option, and the copy for that
option is written to be honest about what it is rather than to punish it.

It also cannot tell who influenced whom, and does not pretend to.

## Finale

**Mechanism.** Consolidation, and self-relevant retrieval.

**Hypothesis.** Four separate lessons decay faster than one story in which they
interact. Ending on a decision that draws on all four is more retrievable than
a summary of all four.

**Objective.** Connect urgency, norms, system design and peer support into one
situation, and end on something the participant chose.

**Design detail.** Each of the four options leans on one chapter's theme, and
the response names which one the participant reached for. Four themed outcomes
plus one shared closing, rather than a branch tree: enough personalisation for
the ending to feel like theirs, few enough paths to stay authored and
checkable.

**Measurement opportunity.** The distribution of finale themes across a cohort.
Which lever a group of young people reaches for under pressure is genuinely
interesting and nobody currently measures it.

**Evidence status.** Plausible. The consolidation rationale is reasonable and
the design is ours.

**Limitations.** A single decision at the end of eighteen minutes is a weak
measure of anything. Treat the distribution as a conversation starter, not a
finding.

## Follow-ups

**Mechanism.** Retrieval practice, and spaced retrieval for the second one.

**Hypothesis.** Recalling a decision a day later moves it from something you did
once towards something you know. A week later, with no story and no station to
signal it, is the closer test of whether the pattern transfers.

**Objective.** Retention, and re-engagement past the event.

**Design detail.** Aftermath (20 hours) continues the same story. One week later
(168 hours) deliberately does not: it is a new group chat, a different friend,
and no narrative scaffolding. If the pattern only shows up when the story
signals it, the pattern has not transferred.

**Measurement opportunity.** Follow-up open and completion rates. This is the
measure most roadshows cannot produce at all, because there is nothing to come
back to.

**Evidence status.** Grounded for retrieval practice and spacing effects, which
are among the better replicated findings in learning research. Untested at this
interval, in this format, with this audience.

**Limitations.** Opening a follow-up is engagement, not behaviour change. It is
a necessary condition for retention, not evidence of it. And in this build
nothing notifies the participant, so completion rates would also be measuring
how often people happen to open the app.

## What the whole Campaign does not claim

- That playing it reduces crime. An eighteen minute experience does not, and no
  pilot of the size described in `docs/PILOT_PLAN.md` could show it.
- That gamification changes behaviour on its own.
- That any figure currently in the product describes a real population.
- That a participant who chose well in a fiction will choose well in the moment.

What it does claim is narrower and defensible: that a roadshow can be
restructured so participants make decisions rather than receive information,
that peer influence can be made visible to the people experiencing it, and that
the experience can continue after the room empties. Whether any of that changes
what somebody does in a real forty seconds is an open question, and it is the
question a pilot should be designed to answer.
