# Game feel research

Focused research for the pass that segmented SIDEQUEST's stories into
player-controlled beats, gave the Campaign a real map, gave the signature
missions visual identity, and added the Echo collection.

Conducted 26 August 2026 against the build at commit `f43707a`.

Unlike the two research documents before it, this pass started from **user
feedback rather than an audit**. Real people used the product and said it was
too wordy, too flat, and hard to navigate inside the Campaign. The previous
automated audits were green, and they were green because accessibility and
hierarchy tests measure whether a screen is usable. They cannot measure whether
anybody wants to use it.

Every entry states a question, the feedback that prompted it, the evidence, the
implication, the decision, and a confidence level.

Confidence is one of:

- **High**: primary source, directly on point.
- **Medium**: good evidence transferred across a gap, or a design judgement layered on top.
- **Low**: reasoned position, thin direct evidence. Flagged so it can be argued with.

---

## 0. A correction before anything else

**The claim this pass refuses to use:** that young people have an eight second
attention span, shorter than a goldfish.

That figure is not supported. It has no traceable peer-reviewed origin: it
circulated from a 2015 consumer marketing report attributing it to a
statistics aggregator that cited no primary study, and no attention researcher
has reproduced it. Wikipedia's summary of the actual literature puts estimates
for healthy teenagers and adults in the region of *hours*, not seconds, and
notes that measured attention varies with task enjoyment, fatigue and age.

More importantly, it is the wrong diagnosis. If attention were a fixed
eight-second budget, no teenager could finish a film, a match or a boss fight,
and the correct response would be to make everything shorter, including the
parts that matter. That is not what the testers ran out of. They ran out of
patience for **reading that stood between them and something to do.**

The right frame is cognitive load. Sweller's distinction, as summarised
verbatim:

> Extraneous cognitive load is "generated by the manner in which information is
> presented to learners and is under the control of instructional designers."

That is the whole diagnosis in one sentence. The *situations* SIDEQUEST asks
about are inherently complex, and that intrinsic load should stay. What was
wasteful was the presentation: three paragraphs delivered simultaneously before
a single tap, with no speaker, no image, and no pacing. **We were spending the
player's working memory on our layout instead of on the decision.**

This correction is recorded because it changes what gets cut. Under the myth,
you shorten everything. Under cognitive load theory, you shorten the
*presentation* and protect the *substance*.

**Confidence.** High for the framing. The eight-second claim's provenance is
recorded from secondary reporting rather than a primary debunk, because search
access ran out before one could be retrieved; the substantive argument does not
depend on it.

## A. Cognitive load and multimedia learning

### A1. Should story text be delivered in one block or in player-advanced beats?

**Feedback.** "Too many words." "Explanations are too long." "Story content
should arrive in shorter beats." "Users lose attention before reaching
decisions."

**Measured baseline.** `node scripts/text-audit.mjs`, written for this pass,
found nine story blocks of 220 to 280 characters rendered simultaneously, and
ten debrief blocks of 227 to 356. On a 390px phone the first beat of ONE BAD
MINUTE chapter one is three paragraphs, roughly seven lines of body text, then
one small button reading "Keep listening".

**Evidence.** The relevant principle is **segmenting**: presenting a
continuous message in learner-paced parts rather than as one continuous unit.
Mayer and Chandler, "When learning is just a click away: Does simple user
interaction foster deeper understanding of multimedia messages?", *Journal of
Educational Psychology* 93(2) (2001), 390-397, doi:10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390,
is the study that established it: adding a simple click-to-continue between
segments improved understanding relative to the same material presented
continuously. Mayer and Moreno, "Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in
Multimedia Learning", *Educational Psychologist* 38(1) (2003), 43-52,
doi:10.1207/s15326985ep3801_6, lists segmenting among its named load-reduction
methods.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** The fix the testers asked for and the fix the
evidence supports are the same fix, which does not always happen. Crucially,
segmenting is about **learner-controlled pacing**, not about deleting content.
A beat the player advances is better than the same words in a block, even at
identical word count.

**Decision.** `StoryBeat`: one component, player-advanced, used by every
narrative surface. Content was re-segmented rather than merely cut. Where a
sentence carried a real idea it was kept and given its own beat.

**Confidence.** High.

### A2. How many beats before it becomes bureaucracy?

**Feedback.** Implicit, and the prompt names it directly: the fix for long
paragraphs is not forty-seven taps.

**Evidence.** Segmenting research supports learner-paced units, not
maximally-small units. Splitting a single idea across two taps adds interaction
cost without reducing load, since the player has to hold the first half in
working memory while fetching the second. That is the split-attention problem
segmenting is meant to solve, reintroduced by overcorrection.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** The unit is the **idea**, not the sentence. Two
short sentences that form one thought belong in one beat.

**Decision.** "One beat, one idea, one action." Tightly coupled lines stay
together. Tap counts to the first decision are measured before and after and
recorded in `docs/FOCAL_POINT_AUDIT.md`; the target was to keep them roughly
level while cutting what is on screen at once, not to trade reading for tapping.

**Confidence.** Medium-high. The principle is well supported; where exactly to
draw each boundary is authorial judgement.

### A3. Does adding pictures help, or is it decoration?

**Feedback.** "Insufficient focal points." "Missions and Updates can feel
visually dull." "High-quality imagery may help stories feel alive."

**Evidence.** The **coherence principle** cuts the other way from naive
"add pictures": material is understood better when extraneous words, sounds and
images are excluded. An image that does not carry information competes for the
same limited channel as the text it sits next to. The **signalling** principle
supports cues that direct attention to what matters. Together they say: images
that do a job help, images that fill space hurt.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** "Make it look fun" is not a job. "Show who is
speaking", "show the object the decision is about", "show that the environment
changed" are jobs.

**Decision.** The rule written into `CLAUDE.md` and
`docs/VISUAL_ART_DIRECTION.md` is **no visual without a job**, with the valid
jobs enumerated. Every image added in this pass names its job. Nothing
decorative was added, and the temptation to put a hero illustration on every
mission card was declined.

**Confidence.** High.

## B. Interactive narrative

### B1. Should a speaker be visually identified?

**Feedback.** "Character/avatar identity could improve appeal." "The experience
still sometimes feels like reading rather than playing."

**Evidence.** This is signalling applied to dialogue. When three consecutive
paragraphs describe what Ken said, what he implied and who he was looking at,
the reader has to reconstruct the speaker on every line. A persistent speaker
identifier removes that reconstruction. The cost is screen space and one more
thing to render, which the coherence principle says must be paid for by
information delivered.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** A portrait earns its place if it answers "who is
talking" faster than the text does. In a four-character scene it does.

**Decision.** A stylised SVG portrait system with a small closed set of
expressions, rendered next to the speaker's name. Not photorealistic: an
uncanny face is a distraction, and a fictional face implying that appearance
signals criminality is a line this product does not cross. Expression is
**never the only carrier** of information, because that would fail both
accessibility and the no-profiling rule.

**Confidence.** Medium-high.

### B2. Does an expressive guide character help or distract?

**Feedback.** Echo already exists. The question is whether to lean on it.

**Evidence.** Schroeder, Adesope and Gilbert, "How Effective are Pedagogical
Agents for Learning? A Meta-Analytic Review", *Journal of Educational Computing
Research* 49(1) (2013), 1-39, doi:10.2190/ec.49.1.a, is the relevant
meta-analysis. The literature it reviews finds small positive effects overall
and, importantly, strong dependence on how the agent is used: agents help most
when they signal and least when they narrate what is already on screen.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** Echo should not repeat the scene. Echo should mark
transitions the player might otherwise miss, and should stay a narrator rather
than becoming a teacher.

**Decision.** Echo's lines stay short, stay reactive to captured state, and are
not added to any new surface where the screen already says the thing. Echo is
still banned from Safe.

**Confidence.** Medium. The meta-analysis is on learning outcomes in
instructional media, which is a different task from a branching narrative.

## C. Adolescent motivation and identity

### C1. Does avatar customisation actually do anything?

**Feedback.** "Character/avatar identity could improve appeal."

**Evidence.** Birk, Atkins, Bowey and Mandryk, "Fostering Intrinsic Motivation
through Avatar Identification in Digital Games", *CHI 2016*, 2982-2995,
doi:10.1145/2858036.2858062. The work ties avatar **identification** to
intrinsic motivation and to autonomy, competence and relatedness, which is the
same self-determination framework the previous pass used. Customisation is one
route to identification, not the only one, and the effect runs through
identification rather than through the number of options.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** A large customisation surface is not where the value
is. A small number of variants that mean something is.

**Decision.** **Echo collection**, not an avatar builder. Five variants, each
unlocked by an action that actually happened, selectable, persisted, and shown
where Echo already appears. No marketplace, no currency, no random drops, no
scarcity, no purchase. Deliberately not a human avatar builder: representation
in a Singapore youth product is a serious design problem and a badly done
inclusive avatar system is worse than none.

**Confidence.** Medium-high for the direction. Low for the specific five.

### C2. Does unlocking things make it a slot machine?

**Evidence.** The previous pass's finding stands: Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa
(2014) found gamification effects "greatly dependent on the context in which
the gamification is being implemented, as well as on the users using it".
Random reward schedules are the mechanism behind the manipulative end of that
range.

**Decision.** Every unlock is **deterministic and legible**: complete this
thing, get that variant. The user can see what is locked and what unlocks it
before doing it. Nothing is random, nothing expires, nothing is bought.

**Confidence.** High.

## D. Sound

### D1. Should there be background music?

**Feedback.** "Audio/music could potentially improve atmosphere."

**Evidence.** Perham and Currie, "Does listening to preferred music improve
reading comprehension performance?", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 28(2)
(2014), 279-284, doi:10.1002/acp.2994. The relevant result from this line of
work is that music with lyrics impairs reading comprehension, and that liking
the music does not rescue it: preference changes how people *feel* about the
task, not how well they perform it.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** SIDEQUEST's core interaction is reading dialogue and
making a decision about it. Background music with lyrics is therefore
disqualified outright. Instrumental ambience is defensible in principle, but it
would need to be sourced, licensed, hosted and lazy-loaded, and the testers'
actual complaint was about reading volume rather than silence.

**Decision.** **No background music.** Short, optional, synthesised interface
sounds only, generated with the Web Audio API so there is no audio file, no
download weight, and no licence to document. Off by default, one visible
control, preference remembered, never in Safe, never carrying information that
is not also on screen. This is the "implement the architecture and a small set
of original UI sounds" option the brief allows, chosen deliberately over
shipping mediocre licensed loops.

**Confidence.** High for excluding lyrics. Medium for excluding ambience
entirely, which is a scope call as much as an evidence call.

## E. Focal hierarchy and navigation

### E1. Why is the Campaign hard to navigate?

**Feedback.** "Mission chapters are not immediately obvious to navigate." This
was the most specific complaint received.

**Evidence.** Signalling again, plus the plain observation from the baseline
screenshots: the Campaign screen listed four chapters as visually equal rows,
each with a status word, inside a screen that also carried a description
paragraph, two mode cards, a progress bar, a station code form, a follow-up
list and an organiser link. Nothing was dominant, so "what do I do next"
required reading everything and deciding.

**SIDEQUEST implication.** A progression screen has exactly one job: say where
you are and what is next. Everything else is secondary by definition.

**Decision.** A story map with visually distinct node states, the current
chapter dominant, and one primary control that names the chapter it will open.
States are distinguished by **shape, icon, label and position, never by colour
alone**, which is both an accessibility requirement and what makes the map
readable at a glance.

**Confidence.** High.

### E2. What is the test for "clear enough"?

**Decision.** The two-second test, applied to a screenshot of every major
screen and recorded in `docs/FOCAL_POINT_AUDIT.md`: shown for about two
seconds, can a person say what the obvious next action is? Hierarchy is fixed
with size, position, contrast and whitespace. **Never with motion.** A pulsing
element that draws the eye is a hierarchy failure wearing a costume, and the
previous pass already banned permanent motion on the one control where it was
most tempting.

**Confidence.** High.

## F. Motion

### F1. What is motion allowed to do?

**Feedback.** "Small animations could make the product feel more playful."

**Evidence.** The previous pass established the rule that nothing may be
revealed by motion or hidden without it, so that the reduced-motion path is
identical rather than merely acceptable. Nothing here revises that. What
motion can legitimately add is *continuity*: showing that this state became
that state, rather than that a different screen appeared.

**Decision.** One-shot transitions on state changes that a player is meant to
notice: a beat arriving, a chapter unlocking, a choice locking in, a collection
item unlocking. No loops, no particles, no permanent mascot motion, no
animated backgrounds. Every one respects `prefers-reduced-motion`, and the
existing global CSS rule already neutralises transitions under it.

**Confidence.** High.

## Decisions this research argued against

Recorded so they are not silently revisited.

| Idea | Why it was dropped |
| ---- | ------------------ |
| Justifying anything with the eight-second attention span | Not supported, and the wrong diagnosis. Section 0. |
| Cutting text everywhere to hit a word count | Segmenting is about pacing, not deletion. Cutting substance would make the decisions shallower. A1. |
| One tap per sentence | Reintroduces split attention and adds interaction cost for no load reduction. A2. |
| A hero illustration on every mission card | Coherence principle. An image without a job competes with the text beside it. A3. |
| Photorealistic character faces | Uncanny, and a fictional face implying appearance signals criminality is a line this product does not cross. B1. |
| A human avatar builder | Representation is a serious design problem and a bad inclusive system is worse than none. C1. |
| Instrumental background music | Defensible in principle, but unsourced, unlicensed and not what the testers were actually missing. D1. |
| Lyrical background music | Impairs reading comprehension, and preference does not rescue it. D1. |
| Random or purchasable Echo variants | Random schedules are the manipulative end of the gamification range. C2. |
| Motion to fix hierarchy | A hierarchy failure wearing a costume. E2. |
| Video scenes | Network weight and production burden for a roadshow PWA, against a text problem that segmenting already solves. |
